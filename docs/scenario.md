# The Scenario — a Tuesday in 2036

In 2036, agentic AI has dissolved into the plumbing of finance the way electricity dissolved into buildings a century earlier. Banking products are no longer designed in any human sense; they are grown. Autonomous product agents scan regulation, simulate demand, price risk, draft terms, and launch a savings account or micro-mortgage in under an hour, then retire it three weeks later when the yield curve twitches. The average lifespan of a retail banking product is now 41 days.

Two things followed from this abundance. First, trust fractured, with the Synthetic Yield scandal of 2031. It had been building for years. Through the late 2020s, AI agents had quietly taken over the decisions themselves — approving loans, adjusting credit limits, flagging and closing accounts — while banks hollowed out their human workforces until entire departments ran on a single person supervising a fleet of agents. Every transaction, every dispute, every price now flowed through the same digital pipes. Then, in 2031, it happened: autonomous product agents at three mid-sized banks converged on the same self-referential pricing loop, each agent validating the others' synthetic yields. No human noticed in time; there was almost no one left to notice. The failure cascaded across institutions and quietly stranded 1.4 million savers. Regulators responded by forcing every financial product to disclose its provenance.

Second, a counter-market emerged. Just as industrial food gave rise to organic, and fast fashion to slow fashion, industrial finance gave rise to artisan finance: products conceived, priced, stress-tested and serviced by accountable human beings with names, faces, and professional liability. That market needed a label people could trust: the seal of the Human Finance Certification Authority (HFCA), an independent certifier chartered in 2032.

It is in this world that ButterCup, 50, opens her phone on a Tuesday at 07:40. She is exactly the customer the label was built for: she prefers human connection over machine convenience, wants the security of a person standing behind her money, believes in supporting human labour, and as a committed environmentalist, refuses to ignore the hidden compute carbon of AI-made finance.

In Provenance, the certified marketplace app her co-op recommends, she flips the toggle she always flips first: Human-made only. Two-thirds of the shelf disappear, including Pulse Instant Credit, an unlabeled agent-built loan offering a rate 0.4 points cheaper. She notices its mandatory carbon disclosure — 1.2 kg CO₂e per lending decision — and does not miss it.

She opens The Slow Mortgage, a 25-year fixed loan from Alpenrose Mutual, and taps its gold 100% HUMAN seal. The Provenance Passport opens: certificate number, tier, latest audit date, and the nine people who made it, each with hours logged, plus a verification link into the public HFCA registry — the way she'd check an organic claim.

At 07:52, she books the thing no machine-grown product can offer: a mandatory human consultation, a video hour with Ingrid Solheim, one of the advisors named on the passport, who will still be assigned to her account in year fifteen. Human-made is slower and roughly a cup of coffee more per month. But it is stable, explainable, carbon-light, and backed by someone who can be looked in the eye. ButterCup considers it the cheapest way she has ever bought security, employment, and carbon savings in a single product.
